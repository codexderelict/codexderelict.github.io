The Sweet, Sweet, Bitter Lesson: What (I Think) The Success of Modern Machine Learning Means for Linguistics

November 26th, 2026

All that I say is based upon my own opinions and understanding. I am not a linguist nor an ML researcher. If you are, and need to correct me for a detail I've gotten wrong, my email is codexderelict@proton.me, and I'm free to conversation anytime, though I may be slow sometimes in response.

The Bitter Lesson by Richard Sutton seems to be one of those essays that's so often spoken of yet so few seem to have read it, and even fewer seem to have grasped it. When he recently Tweeted "Brute force computation is not enough for intelligence. Clever new methods are needed.", many responded with "But what about the Bitter Lesson?". It's a shame, because it's two pages and written in the simplest prose possible, but I digress.

To quote Sutton: "The actual contents of minds are tremendously, irredeemably complex; we should stop trying to find simple ways to think about the contents of minds...They are not what should be built in, as their complexity is endless; instead we should build in only the meta-methods that can find and capture this arbitrary complexity." I like to think about it as the "shape" of a problem, and then find an architecture that can instantiate that shape, rather than giving the program the shape ourselves. This is especially relevant given more recent developments in automated theorem proving and the potential for statistical models to outperform symbolic ones hand-crafted by mathematical logicians who can spot a bad proof from Saturn's orbit.

Where do linguists come into the fray? After all, as Sutton underscores, more "shallow" and encoded-knowledge-lean statistical methods seem to have outperformed encoded-knowledge-rich symbolic systems built by linguists in natural language and speech processing. Intuitively speaking, this spells bad news for linguists. After all, it seems as though the wealth of human knowledge about linguistic theory, from Saussure to Bloomfield to Chomsky to Pullum to Whorf, has been very well outperformed by machines that seem to only have been built with little of that knowledge. MIT psycholinguist Ted Gibson, in his 2025 book on syntax, even states: "In any case, [LLMs] are far better than any preceding linguistic theory at explaining real language data."

Alright, linguists, let's pack it up, put your notebooks in your bags, erase that constituency tree from the blackboard, and get that declension chart out of here, tell your human subjects hooked up to that EEG to buzz off and eat one, the machines are at the door! Or, wait, actually, come back for a minute, your work isn't done here. Let the machines in! Behold, a new subject! Clean as a whistle, billions of parameters and weights, ask it a question, let it read you a bedtime story, and watch as it handles the twisted contours of natural language syntax with ease! You might be impressed, and you might not. If you are, you're in very good company. If you're not, well, you're in the company of one Noam Chomsky.

After all, this was the guy that desired to move linguistics past pure corpus analysis and linguistic behaviors and patterns and into what innate machinery exists to construct the infinitude of human syntax, and has been doing that since the 1950s. Here's what he had to say, in an interview with Ramin Mirfakhraie from 2023: "Even the most superficial familiarity reveals that very young children already have rich command of basic principles of their language, far beyond what they exhibit in performance...It is absurd beyond discussion to believe that any light can be shed on these processes by LLMs, which scan astronomical amounts of data to find statistical regularities that allow fair prediction of the next likely word in a sequence based on the enormous corpus they analyze."

This was in response to Steven Piantadosi's 2023 paper, quite aggressively titled "Modern language models refute Chomsky’s approach to language", pointing to complexities such as "multiple embedded clauses, prepositional phrases, conjunctions, etc" in LLMs' outputs, stating generative approaches to syntax "are not competitive in any domain and arguably have avoided empirical tests of their core assumptions" in comparison to the success of LLMs in natural language processing, and arguing that statistical models such as LLMs are more readily integrated "with what we know about other fields, specifically cognition and neuroscience."

So far, Chomsky seems to have a point: with enough data, you can approximate anything, but that might not tell you much about how that thing works. Humans acquire language with far less data than what LLMs have. Piantadosi has a point: given neural networks have had such success in computational neuroscience, wouldn't that just be a golden opportunity to integrate findings from it into linguistics and vice versa and widen the horizon of theoretical linguistic research? There has to be more to it than just those two... and there is! Futrell and Mahowald's 2025 paper "How Linguistics Learned to Stop Worrying and Love the Language Models".

They argue, in short: "While they do not replace linguistic structure and theory, they serve as model systems and working proofs of concept for gradient, usage-based approaches to language." They state that linguists, including those of the generative tradition, have done well in identifying what they call the "Real Patterns" of language, and that their work is indispensable in understanding how neural networks use, represent and process language. And here is when we circle back to Sutton: they argue "Asking how much one can learn ‘from the data alone’ without inductive bias is like asking how close a runner can get to the finish line without saying where they started." In asking what the inductive biases that yield the complexities of natural language are, we also attempt to find the architecture that instantiates the "shape" of natural language.

And now that we've gotten to the question of "inductive biases", we've also gotten to the question of what biological processes specific to humans affect or determine the structure of natural language. Be it in the form of an innate grammar, processing and memory limitations, or the plain old ingenuity of human pattern-matching, we know there's something there, the question remains on how that "something" is shaped.

The "shape" of these inductive biases can be hard to sus out; filler-gap dependencies can be represented with movement among transformational syntacticians, with SLASH in HPSG, with a construction in CxG, and even within those currents there's overlap with "opposing camps" and internal disagreement. No two scientists, including linguists, are alike. Why not compare and contrast them with how they play out in the human parser, or a neural network surrogate?

I'm not alone here either; Tom McCoy's commentary on F&M mentions his work using meta-learning to instantiate a kind of UG in a neural network by showing it "many languages sampled from a space of possible languages", such that it acquires knowledge on what languages are likely or unlikely to exist, and says, in no uncertain terms: "Because LMs can instantiate usage-based theories or generative ones, they create opportunities to evaluate both types of theories."

One slightly disappointing thing is the lack of work with neural networks in theorizing for so-called "performance-compatible competence grammars" (Sag and Wasow 2011), especially given the fact their accounts of generative linguistic competence is far more open to matters of linguistic function and processing, going as far as to say: "We believe not only that grammatical theorists should be interested in performance modelling, but also that empirical facts about various aspects of performance can and should inform the development of the theory of linguistic competence."

But, only time will tell. One big step, I think, is Gibson's aforementioned 2025 book, given he explicitly proposes a constructional formalism partly inspired by HPSG (with dependency grammar, rather than phrase-structure as HPSG uses) and mentions LLMs may instantiate something similar to it, as well as Tom McCoy's implementation of Optimality Theory in neural networks in 2020 (mentioned in the same commentary as an illustration of his point about UG). Another, I think, is the more recent work of Adam Przepiorkowski (such as Patejuk and Przepiorkowski 2025) in using NLP techniques (not LLMs, but neural dependency parsing) to test a symmetric account of coordination, a notoriously controversial phenomenon among syntacticians.

There really is nothing as sweet as Sutton's bitter lesson; not only does it reflect what seems to work well in ML engineering, but it reminds us of explanatory adequacy stretching far beyond language itself, in what biases, specific, general or both, exist that instantiate the "shapes" of natural language and their meta-linguistic representations. But I do have one last worry: general statistical methods sitting in for theory. After all, if the same model can be used in biology, economics, physics, and neuroscience with some degree of predictive success, what does it tell us about language specifically? As is often said, "garbage in, garbage out." A model needs to exist, but what needs to also exist is a hypothesis. I believe that linguistic formalisms, stated in as rigorous of terms as possible, allow for precise hypotheses to be made to inform such a model. But to explicitly encode those formalisms themselves tells us little.

Here's to the future, though, one with a round table of mathematicians, computer scientists, linguists and neuroscientists, united in trying to uncover one of the most bespoke mysteries of human biology, that being the fact we use our hands, tongues and brains to communicate abstract concepts in a manner seemingly isolated to our species (or possibly genus, though we don't know too much about all the others; they died about 40,000 years before the birth of Saussure).
